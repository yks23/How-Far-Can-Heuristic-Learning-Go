\abstract{Reinforcement learning (RL) made its name on adversarial games, and it
is there that symbolic methods --- heuristic rules and search --- were judged to
have the lower ceiling, on the grounds that a program exhibits only the behaviour
someone wrote down. The objection is correct, but it describes the author rather
than the artifact. Coding agents change who the author is, moving the operative
property from interpretability to editability. How far the symbolic paradigm goes
once a machine pays the maintenance cost is a two-sided question. An
agent-maintained policy wins on information per sample --- rules and a replay
report which decision lost a game, a scalar reward only that one was lost ---
then stops, because the program outgrows what the agent can revise without
breaking it. We characterize both
bounds with \emph{heuristic learning} (HL): an agent revises a program from the
rules, its own source, and replays of its losses, rescoring against a frozen
opponent pool. The setting is the complete archive of a collegiate game AI
contest running since \val{founded} --- built to be played rather than evaluated
--- whose \val{nsub} submissions and \val{nmatch} adjudicated matches supply a
distribution of human skill rather than one reference solution.
HL reaches the \val{pct}th percentile of that field across \val{ngames} games and
is \val{effratio} more sample-efficient than model-based RL at matched Elo, but
that baseline overtakes beyond \val{crossover} environment steps, and improvement
halts near
\val{plateau} revisions as the program's description length approaches
\val{ceilingcause}: \val{maintcap}, within \val{maintcapspread} across games of
differing complexity, while the Elo reached there spreads by
\val{eloplatspread} --- equal maintainable size at unequal strength, which makes
the ceiling the agent's and not the game's. What bounds the paradigm is the
description length of a winning program, not the complexity of the rules.}
