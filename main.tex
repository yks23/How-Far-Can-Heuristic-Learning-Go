\title{How Far Can Heuristic Learning Go? \\
Characterizing Performance Ceilings, Complexity, and Sample Efficiency in Large-Scale Real-World Competitive Games}

\author{THU-CST-SAST}

\abstract{Reinforcement learning (RL) made its name on adversarial games, and it
is there that symbolic methods --- heuristic rules and search --- were judged to
have the lower ceiling, on the grounds that a program exhibits only the behaviour
someone wrote down. The objection is correct, but it describes the author rather
than the artifact. Coding agents change who the author is, moving the operative
property from interpretability to editability. How far the symbolic paradigm goes
once a machine pays the maintenance cost has two answers. Symbolic policies win
on information per sample --- rules and a replay report which decision lost a
game, a scalar reward only that one was lost --- then stop, because the program
outgrows what the agent can revise without breaking it. We characterize both bounds with \emph{heuristic learning}
(HL) --- an agent revises a program from the rules, its own source, and replays of
its losses, rescoring against a frozen opponent pool --- on the complete archive
of a collegiate game AI contest running since \val{founded}, built to be played
rather than evaluated, whose \val{nsub} submissions and \val{nmatch} adjudicated
matches supply a distribution of human skill rather than one reference solution.
HL reaches the \val{pct}th percentile of that field across \val{ngames} games and
is \val{effratio} more sample-efficient than RL at matched Elo, but RL overtakes
beyond \val{crossover} environment steps, and improvement halts near
\val{plateau} revisions as the program's description length approaches
\val{ceilingcause}: \val{maintcap}, within \val{maintcapspread} across games of
differing complexity. What bounds the paradigm is the description length of a
winning program, not the complexity of the rules.}
