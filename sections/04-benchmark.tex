\section{\benchname}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\benchname is an evaluation arena built on the archive of a long-running collegiate
game AI contest. It holds \val{ngames} games, each with its rules, a machine-readable
decision space, a runnable referee, and the graded field of human programs that
competed in it; \val{nsubv} archived submission versions in total. It is not a
collection of tasks authored for a benchmark. Every game was designed to be played
competitively by people, every opponent in it is a program somebody wrote to win a
contest, and the rankings it records were produced by that contest rather than by us.
What we add is a uniform way to run those programs against each other and against
methods of our own, described in Section~\ref{sec:arena-eval}.

\subsection{Provenance}
\label{sec:arena-provenance}

The venue is a computer science department's annual agent contest, founded in
\val{founded} and in its \val{editions}th edition. Each edition poses one new game,
publishes its rules and a software development kit, and runs an online ladder over a
semester in which entrants submit programs, play ranked matches, and resubmit; the
strongest entrants advance to a final ladder or an offline final. Two properties of
this arrangement matter for what the archive can be used for. Entrants iterate under
their own effort budget over months, so a submission history is a record of sustained
human maintenance rather than a single attempt. And the contest kept every version
submitted, not only the last, so the archive records the trajectory as well as its
endpoint.

Publicly verifiable material does not extend back to the founding. The platform's own
edition-level index reports that the earliest editions survive only as an inferred list
of years, with no rules, code, standings, or replays, and that the first edition
holding a complete asset set is edition~\val{edfirst}. \benchname therefore spans
editions \val{edfirst}--\val{edlast}: seven consecutive editions, \val{ngames} games,
because two editions posed two games each. The venue's age is what makes the archive
what it is, but it is not the archive's reach, and we quote the two separately.

One consequence is worth stating before it is relied on. The contest predates this
benchmark by decades and was never built for evaluation, so the archive was not
assembled with any model's training data in view. That is a property this venue has
for free, where benchmarks that need it otherwise buy it by authoring fresh tasks or
collecting them continuously~\citep{livecodebench2024,workbuddybench2026}. It is not
proof of anything: the archive is a public web artifact, and provenance alone cannot
establish that a model has not seen these games. What it licenses is a contamination
check rather than an assumption, and we specify one below.

The suite is not uniform, and three departures constrain what may be claimed over it.

\subsection{What the archive records}
\label{sec:arena-archive}

Every submission version is kept as an independent entry: a directory of the entrant's
source as submitted, together with a manifest row giving the entrant, the version
index, the contest track, and the finals rank and rating where the ladder recorded
them. The unit is the version, not the person. The \val{nsubv} versions come from
\val{nusers} distinct entrants, about thirty-five versions each, so the field is small
and strongly correlated and any placement computed over versions has \val{nusers} as
its effective sample size at most. Of the registered versions, \val{nsubcode} have
source on disk and are playable; the remainder are registered without code and are
excluded from every pool. The archive arrives with a fixed split, \val{ntrain}
versions for development and \val{ntest} held out, which we adopt as given rather than
resampling.

Two limits of the recorded metadata bear directly on the human comparison. First, the
pools are finals ladders rather than every entrant, so \val{nrank} versions carry a
rank and the field per game is on the order of twenty finalists with many versions
each. A percentile against this field is a percentile of a finalist field: a stronger
opponent set than the full entry list and a much smaller one, and we say so wherever a
percentile appears. Second, the ratings are not on one scale. \val{nelo} versions
carry a rating at all, and the values come from whatever each edition's ladder
computed, with one game's ratings recorded on an unrelated scale and another's absent
entirely. We therefore treat the archive's ratings as provenance rather than
measurement, and recompute all ratings used in this paper with our own referee under
the protocol below.

A third caveat concerns the submissions as programs. Entrants ship the contest SDK and
often third-party libraries inside their submission directory, so a directory's size
is policy plus scaffolding plus vendored dependencies, and the vendored share differs
by orders of magnitude across games. Any measurement of a program's size --
Section~\ref{sec:hl-motivation}'s description length in particular -- must strip these
before comparing games, and the stripping protocol is part of the measurement rather
than a preprocessing detail.

\subsection{Evaluation logic}
\label{sec:arena-eval}

The arena exposes one referee interface for all \val{ngames} games. A match is
requested as a game, an ordered list of players, the roles they occupy, and a seed, and
it returns the winner, a score per role, the number of rounds, and a path to a replay.
Games differ in how they adjudicate; nothing above the interface knows how, so a method
under test is a player and nothing else. Fixing the seed together with the players and
their roles fixes the match: the same request reproduces the same outcome and the same
replay, and the replay records the referee and backend versions that produced it. Player
code runs isolated under time and memory limits and cannot read its opponent's source
or the referee's internal state. Where the roles are not symmetric -- unequal starting
positions, or the two sides of an asymmetric game -- every pairing is played in both
role orders on paired seeds, and only the balanced result is used.

Outcomes are separated into three states, and the separation is what makes ratings from
this arena trustworthy. A match either completes, or fails inside the rules, or fails in
the infrastructure. Failure inside the rules is the player's: a timeout, an illegal
move, a crash of the submitted program, all adjudicated as a loss under the game's own
rules and all counted. Failure of the infrastructure is not the player's, is never
recorded as a loss, and is rerun. Conflating the two silently transfers the arena's
own faults onto whichever method happens to be slower or heavier, which for a
comparison between a compiled human submission and an agent-driven pipeline is not a
neutral error. When attribution is unclear the match is marked infrastructure failure
and reviewed rather than charged to the player.

A replay is a full per-decision record, not an outcome log, and this is the property
Section~\ref{sec:hl-bottleneck} identified as setting the ceiling on what any revision
can be held responsible for. Every frame carries the acting role, the public state it
faced, the set of legal moves available in that state, the move it chose, and whether
the referee accepted it. So the trace answers, at each decision, what the player could
see, what it could have done, and what it did. That is what makes a loss attributable
to a rule in the program rather than to the game as a whole, and it is the input the
loop of Section~\ref{sec:hl-method} consumes. The record respects the game's own
information boundary: hidden information never enters a public state, and under
imperfect information each role's view is recorded separately. Moves the referee
rejected are recorded but marked, and excluded from the player's decision sequence.

Runs report through one schema so that results from different methods and different
games are commensurable. Per-iteration events carry the candidate under test, the
reference it is scored against, behavioural change measured at the decision level, and
three separate budgets: what the method spent learning, what it spent being evaluated,
and the total, each in tokens and wall-clock. Reporting learning and evaluation cost
apart is what makes a sample-efficiency comparison possible at all, since a method that
learns cheaply by evaluating exhaustively has not saved anything. Two conventions in
the schema are load-bearing and we follow them throughout. Change in what a policy
chooses and change in which states it reaches are recorded as separate quantities and
never summed, because a policy can be edited without changing where it goes and can go
somewhere new without being edited. And an unmeasured quantity is recorded as missing
rather than as zero: zero is a measurement, and the distinction is what keeps an
unevaluated run from averaging into a result.
