\section{\benchname}
\label{sec:benchmark}

\benchname is an evaluation arena built on the archive of a long-running collegiate
game AI contest. It holds \val{ngames} games, each with its rules, a machine-readable
decision space, a runnable referee, and the graded field of human programs that
competed in it; \val{nsubv} archived submission versions in total. It is not a
collection of tasks authored for a benchmark. Every game was designed to be played
competitively by people, every opponent in it is a program somebody wrote to win a
contest, and the rankings it records were produced by that contest rather than by us.
What we add is a uniform way to run those programs against each other and against
methods of our own, described in Section~\ref{sec:arena-eval}.

\subsection{Provenance}
\label{sec:arena-provenance}

The venue is a computer science department's annual agent contest, founded in
\val{founded} and in its \val{editions}th edition. Each edition poses one new game,
publishes its rules and a development kit, and runs an online ladder over a semester in
which entrants submit, play ranked matches, and resubmit; the strongest advance to a
final ladder. Two properties of this arrangement are what the archive is for. Entrants
iterate under their own effort budget over months, so a submission history records
sustained human maintenance rather than one attempt. And every version submitted was
kept, not only the last, so the archive holds trajectories and not just endpoints.

Verifiable material does not reach back to the founding: the platform's own
edition-level index reports the earliest editions surviving only as an inferred list of
years, and the first edition with a complete asset set is edition~\val{edfirst}.
\benchname therefore spans editions \val{edfirst}--\val{edlast} --- seven editions,
\val{ngames} games, two editions having posed two each. The venue's age is what makes
the archive what it is but is not the archive's reach, and we quote the two separately.
Because the contest predates this benchmark by decades and was never built for
evaluation, the archive was not assembled with any model's training data in view, where
benchmarks needing that property otherwise buy it by authoring tasks
continuously~\citep{livecodebench2024,workbuddybench2026}. Provenance is not proof: the
archive is a public web artifact, so what it licenses is a contamination check rather
than an assumption.

Table~\ref{tab:games} lists the suite --- a hex-grid tower defence, a snake-and-go
territory hybrid, a maze pursuit, a resource-and-terrain war game, a four-player
survival scramble, a hidden-identity card battle, a scripted interrogation --- with
rules running from a handful of operations to several interacting subsystems, which is
what makes it usable for asking whether a limit belongs to the method or to the game.
Appendix~\ref{app:arena} gives the per-game archive breakdown and the two game
directories excluded from the suite; Appendix~\ref{app:case} works one game's decision
space and replay through in full.

The suite varies, and the variation both widens what a claim covers and limits what it can

\subsection{Contents}
\label{sec:arena-archive}

Every submission version is kept as an independent entry: the entrant's source as
submitted, plus a manifest row giving the entrant, the version index, the track, and the
finals rank and rating where the ladder recorded them. The unit is the version, not the
person, and the two must not be conflated. \val{nrownamed} versions carry a plaintext
entrant identity, from \val{nusersnamed} distinct people; the remaining \val{nrowanon}
are anonymised, and their identities resolve to at most \val{nusersanon} distinct
hashes which cannot be linked across games or to the named entrants. The field is
therefore small and strongly correlated relative to its version count, and any placement
computed over versions must state its unit of independence. Of the registered versions
\val{nsubcode} have source on disk and are playable; the rest are registered without
code and excluded from every pool. The archive arrives with a fixed split,
\val{ntrain} development and \val{ntest} held out, which we adopt as given.

The version index is the archive's most useful record, because the platform numbers each
entrant's resubmissions: a row's index is the number of times that person had revised
their program. Human effort is thus measurable in the same unit as ours --- revisions,
not months. Across the \val{nrank} ranked versions the median placing entry is revision
\val{humanmedrev}, the upper quartile \val{humanq3rev}, and the deepest
\val{humanmaxrev}. Appendix~\ref{app:human} reports this per game and states the
caveats; it is the reference \val{hlspan} is set against.

Two limits of the metadata bear on that comparison. The pools are finals ladders rather
than every entrant, so all \val{nrank} ranked versions fall within the named entrants
and a percentile against them is a percentile of a finalist field --- a stronger
opponent set than the full entry list and a much smaller one. And the ratings are not
one scale: \val{nelo} versions carry a rating, computed by whatever each edition's
ladder used, one game's on an unrelated scale and another's absent. We treat the
archive's ratings as provenance and recompute every rating in this paper with our own
referee. Separately, entrants ship the contest SDK and often third-party libraries
inside their submission directory, and the vendored share differs across games by
orders of magnitude, so any measurement of program size ---
Section~\ref{sec:hl-motivation}'s description length in particular --- must strip these
first, as part of the measurement rather than as preprocessing.

\subsection{Protocol}
\label{sec:arena-eval}

The arena exposes one referee interface for all \val{ngames} games. A match is
requested as a game, an ordered list of players, the roles they occupy, and a seed, and
returns the winner, a score per role, the number of rounds, and a replay. Games differ
in how they adjudicate and nothing above the interface knows how, so a method under
test is a player and nothing else. Fixing the seed with the players and their roles
fixes the match: the same request reproduces the same outcome and the same replay,
which records the referee and backend versions that produced it. Player code runs
isolated under time and memory limits and cannot read its opponent's source or the
referee's internal state. Where roles are not symmetric --- unequal starting positions,
or the two sides of an asymmetric game --- every pairing is played in both role orders
on paired seeds and only the balanced result is used.

Outcomes are separated into three states, and the separation is what makes ratings from
this arena trustworthy. A match either completes, or fails inside the rules, or fails in
the infrastructure. Failure inside the rules is the player's: a timeout, an illegal
move, a crash of the submitted program, all adjudicated as a loss under the game's own
rules and all counted. Failure of the infrastructure is not the player's, is never
recorded as a loss, and is rerun. Conflating the two silently transfers the arena's
own faults onto whichever method happens to be slower or heavier, which for a
comparison between a compiled human submission and an agent-driven pipeline is not a
neutral error. When attribution is unclear the match is marked infrastructure failure
and reviewed rather than charged to the player.

A replay is a full per-decision record rather than an outcome log, which is the property
Section~\ref{sec:hl-bottleneck} identified as setting the ceiling on what any revision
can be held responsible for. Every frame carries the acting role, the public state it
faced, the legal moves available there, the move it chose, and whether the referee
accepted it, so the trace answers at each decision what the player could see, what it
could have done, and what it did. That is what makes a loss attributable to a rule in
the program rather than to the game as a whole, and it is the $\tau_t$ the loop of
Section~\ref{sec:hl-method} consumes. The record respects the game's information
boundary: hidden state never enters a public observation, and under imperfect
information each role's view is recorded separately. Rejected moves are recorded but
marked and excluded from the decision sequence. Appendix~\ref{app:case} shows a frame
in full.

Runs report through one schema so results from different methods and games are
commensurable. Per-iteration events carry the candidate, the reference it is scored
against, behavioural change measured at the decision level, and three separate budgets:
what the method spent learning, what it spent being evaluated, and the total, each in
tokens and wall-clock. Separating learning from evaluation cost is what makes a
sample-efficiency comparison possible at all, since a method that learns cheaply by
evaluating exhaustively has saved nothing. Two conventions are load-bearing and we
follow them throughout. Change in what a policy chooses and change in which states it
reaches are recorded separately and never summed, because a policy can be edited
without changing where it goes and can go somewhere new without being edited. And an
unmeasured quantity is recorded as missing rather than zero, since zero is itself a
measurement --- the distinction is what keeps an unevaluated run from averaging into a
result. Appendix~\ref{app:apparatus} gives both contracts.
