\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Adversarial games were first won by symbolic construction. A hand-authored
evaluation function over a search procedure is what defeated a reigning world chess
champion \citep{deepblue2002}, and for decades that construction --- rules, an
evaluation function, a search --- was how a competitive game program was built.
Reinforcement learning (RL) then displaced it on the same terrain. Chess, Go, shogi,
StarCraft, and Dota are where learned policies beat the strongest humans alive, and
where the field's characteristic result took shape: a policy trained by self-play,
given enough interaction, exceeds anything a person wrote by hand
\citep{alphazero2018,alphastar2019,openaifive2019}. What separates the two paradigms
is how each one improves. A learned policy improves by continuing to play, and the
adversary supplies the signal indefinitely. A symbolic program improves only when
somebody revises it: every new case has to be noticed, encoded, and reconciled with
the rules already present, at a cost that grows with the program until further
revision stops being worth its price. That maintenance cost, and not any limit on
what a program can express, is what bounded the paradigm.

The cost, though, is a property of the author rather than of the artifact. Per unit of
construction the symbolic paradigm is if anything the more expressive of the two: a
search procedure or a conditional test states in one line a decision boundary that
takes a great many neurons to approximate. What binds is how much of such a program a
human will maintain before the reconciliation cost exceeds what they can pay.
Asymptotically the learned policy still wins, and this paper does not dispute that.
The question is what happens under a budget, where the authoring cost binds --- and
because that question is about games, this paper stays on the terrain where RL is
strongest.

Coding agents change who the author is. Once the author is a machine, the property
that governs whether revision can continue is \emph{editability} rather than
interpretability: interpretability asks whether a
human understands the policy, editability whether the optimization loop can act on
the artifact at all. A symbolic program can be read in full and changed in part, and
takes the heterogeneous inputs maintenance requires --- rules, its own source,
replays of losses. A learned policy admits no comparable loop: its behaviour lives in
weights that cannot be edited locally and offer no handle by which a specific failure
traces to a specific parameter. The two properties do come apart --- a system can
reason in natural language about every decision and still hold its policy in weights
\citep{tig2025think} --- and programmatic RL already calls its policies human-readable
and editable \citep[DiPRL;][]{diprl2026}. What changes here is the role the property
plays: not a feature of the delivered artifact, but the reason an optimizer can exist
for it at all. Once a coding agent is that optimizer --- the engine driving revision
of a symbolic program, in place of the human whose cost bounded the paradigm --- the
question the paradigm was retired on becomes askable again, and quantitatively: how
high is the ceiling?

We study it through \emph{heuristic learning} (HL): a coding agent maintains a heuristic program
by reading the rules, its own source, and replays of its losses, then rescores it
against a frozen opponent pool. The loop is deliberately narrow --- one revision per
iteration, every version and score kept in an append-only log --- because attribution
is the property we are trying to preserve: without it a plateau can be observed but
not diagnosed. A stronger model alone does not supply that. In the closest existing
head-to-head humans took the top five places, and a frontier model handed the winning
student's program dropped it to tenth \citep{vibecoding2025tournament}; those agents
were not one-shot --- most were revised and all debugged over many cycles --- but the
loop optimized compilability, not tournament standing. The missing term is revisions
carrying an adversarial signal.

Measuring a ceiling needs a domain where many independent authors already pushed the
symbolic paradigm as far as a real budget allowed, with the results recorded, so we
use the complete archive of a collegiate game AI contest running since \val{founded}:
games authored for the contest itself, \val{nsub} submissions, and \val{nmatch}
adjudicated matches, written under deadline with standings at stake. That provenance
supplies contamination resistance and a \emph{distribution} of human skill to place a
method within (Section~\ref{sec:preliminary}), and it records a folk ordering --- rules
for beginners, search for the intermediate, RL as the highest ceiling --- that gets
the asymptotic ceilings right and still fails to predict who wins: \val{pheur} of
prize-winning programs contain no learned component, and RL, present in \val{prl} of
submissions, reached the finals in \val{nrlfinal} of \val{editions} editions. Running
HL there and in the classic RL environments, where learned baselines are established,
we find it strongest exactly where a budget binds --- the \val{pct}th percentile of
the human field, \val{effratio} the sample efficiency of model-based RL at matched
Elo, and the largest margins on games whose winning policy is short. That last
regularity names the axis, which is a description length and not the length of the
rules: Go's rules fit on a page, yet no compact symbolic policy plays it well. What
governs the outcome is the description length of a \emph{symbolic} policy good enough
to win, written $K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$ after Kolmogorov complexity and estimated by
compression, the quantity itself being uncomputable
\citep{lividanyi2019kolmogorov,rissanen1978mdl}, and the prediction is a single
inequality: HL succeeds while $K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$ stays below the agent's
maintenance capacity and plateaus as it approaches it. Because that capacity belongs
to the agent rather than the game, the inequality predicts a signature we then
observe --- description length at the plateau converges across games of differing
complexity while the Elo reached there does not, and it is the pairing, not either
half, that separates a limit of the maintainer from a limit of the game
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Neither paradigm dominates: model-based RL overtakes
HL beyond \val{crossover} environment steps, so the budget and the game's structure
decide, while the loop itself transfers to \val{ntransfer} further benchmarks without
retuning.

Two objections are conceded here and answered in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The
agent arrives with a pretraining corpus while RL starts cold, and no denominator makes
that symmetric; we report environment steps as what is scarce in a new domain and test
what pretraining supplies (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}). And the archive's RL
scarcity is confounded with its participants' cost structure --- students on a deadline
have limited compute, time, and RL expertise --- so it records what wins under contest
constraints. Every comparison here is budget-conditional.

Our contributions are threefold.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{We formally define heuristic learning}, an agent-maintained symbolic
  policy under adversarial pressure, instrumented --- one revision per iteration,
  every version and score logged --- so that a plateau can be diagnosed and not
  merely observed.

  \item \textbf{We introduce a benchmark drawn from a real competition} rather than
  built for evaluation: the archive's \val{nsub} submissions and \val{nmatch}
  matches, plus the apparatus one needs --- classic games as structural controls,
  three opponent pools, a frozen evaluation set, a cross-edition Elo protocol, and a
  per-submission audit of whether it contains a learned component.

  \item \textbf{We compare HL and RL broadly} --- against model-based RL, policy
  gradient, and the human field, across budgets and in every denominator rather than
  the favourable one --- and establish which paradigm holds the advantage where. HL
  beats the strongest recorded human on \val{nbeat} games but costs more
  \val{loseaxis}, and improvement frequency decays as \val{decay} to a plateau near
  \val{plateau} revisions, which we trace to maintenance capacity: description length
  there converges to \val{maintcap} within \val{maintcapspread} while the Elo reached
  varies by \val{eloplatspread}. From that we derive practical guidelines for
  applying HL --- when to expect it to pay, and when a learned method is the better
  spend (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{itemize}
