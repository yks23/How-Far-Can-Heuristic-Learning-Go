\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) established itself on adversarial games. Chess, Go,
shogi, StarCraft, and Dota are where learned policies first beat the strongest humans
alive, and where the field's characteristic result took shape: a policy trained by
self-play, given enough interaction, exceeds anything a person wrote by hand
\citep{alphazero2018,alphastar2019,openaifive2019}. Games are also where the
symbolic alternative --- rules, an evaluation function, a search procedure --- was
judged to have the lower ceiling, so this paper stays on that turf.

The objection is that a program exhibits only the behaviour somebody wrote down:
every new case has to be noticed, encoded, and reconciled with the rules already
present, at a cost that grows with the program. This is correct and we do not dispute
it --- but it is a claim about the author, not the artifact. Nothing in the symbolic
paradigm caps what a program can express; the cap is on how much of it a human will
maintain before the reconciliation cost exceeds what they can pay. Asymptotically the
learned policy still wins; the question is what happens under a budget, where the
authoring cost binds.

\paragraph{What coding agents changed.} They change who the author is, and the
property that matters once the author is a machine is not the one usually argued. The
axis is \emph{editability}, not interpretability: interpretability asks whether a
human understands the policy, editability whether the optimization loop can act on
the artifact at all. A symbolic program can be read in full and changed in part, and
takes the heterogeneous inputs maintenance requires --- rules, its own source,
replays of losses. A learned policy admits no comparable loop: its behaviour lives in
weights that cannot be edited locally and offer no handle by which a specific failure
traces to a specific parameter. The two properties do come apart --- a system can
reason in natural language about every decision and still hold its policy in weights
\citep{tig2025think} --- and programmatic RL already calls its policies human-readable
and editable \citep[DiPRL;][]{diprl2026}. What changes here is the role the property
plays: not a feature of the delivered artifact, but the reason an optimizer exists
for it.

\paragraph{Heuristic learning.} So we were curious how the paradigm actually
performs on adversarial games once a machine pays the maintenance cost. We study it
through \emph{heuristic learning} (HL): a coding agent maintains a heuristic program
by reading the rules, its own source, and replays of its losses, then rescores it
against a frozen opponent pool. The loop is deliberately narrow --- one revision per
iteration, every version and score kept in an append-only log --- because attribution
is the property we are trying to preserve: without it a plateau can be observed but
not diagnosed. A stronger model alone does not supply that. In the closest existing
head-to-head humans took the top five places, and a frontier model handed the winning
student's program dropped it to tenth \citep{vibecoding2025tournament}; those agents
were not one-shot --- most were revised and all debugged over many cycles --- but the
loop optimized compilability, not tournament standing. The missing term is revisions
carrying an adversarial signal.

\paragraph{Where we test it.} Measuring a ceiling needs a domain where many
independent authors already pushed the symbolic paradigm as far as a real budget
allowed, with the results recorded. We use the complete archive of a collegiate game
AI contest running since \val{founded}: games authored for the contest itself,
\val{nsub} submissions, and \val{nmatch} adjudicated matches, written under deadline
with standings at stake. That provenance, rather than any design choice, is what
supplies contamination resistance and a \emph{distribution} of human skill to place a
method within (Section~\ref{sec:background}). It also carries a folk ordering ---
rules for beginners, search for the intermediate, RL as the highest ceiling --- which
gets the asymptotic ceilings right and still fails to predict who wins: \val{pheur}
of prize-winning programs contain no learned component, and RL, present in \val{prl}
of submissions, reached the finals in \val{nrlfinal} of \val{editions} editions. We
run HL in the classic RL game environments as well, so the comparison also happens
where learned policies are strongest and their baselines are established.

\paragraph{What we find.} HL is at its strongest exactly where a budget binds. It
reaches the \val{pct}th percentile of the human field and is \val{effratio} more
sample-efficient than model-based RL at matched Elo, while its advantage is largest on
games whose winning policy is short. The axis that predicts this is a description
length, and not the length of the rules --- Go's rules fit on a page, yet no compact
symbolic policy plays it well. What governs the outcome is the description length of a
\emph{symbolic} policy good enough to win, which we write $K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$
after Kolmogorov complexity and estimate by compression, the quantity itself being
uncomputable \citep{lividanyi2019kolmogorov,rissanen1978mdl}. The prediction is a
single inequality: HL succeeds while $K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$ stays below the agent's
maintenance capacity, and plateaus as it approaches it. Because that capacity belongs
to the agent rather than the game, the inequality predicts a signature we then
observe: description length at the plateau converges across games of differing
complexity while the Elo reached there does not, and it is that pairing, not either
half, that separates a limit of the maintainer from a limit of the game
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Neither paradigm dominates --- model-based RL
overtakes HL beyond \val{crossover} environment steps --- so the budget and the
game's structure decide. The loop itself proves robust across that range: it
transfers to \val{ntransfer} further benchmarks without retuning.

Two objections are conceded here and answered in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. The
agent arrives with a pretraining corpus while RL starts cold, and no denominator makes
that symmetric; we report environment steps as what is scarce in a new domain and test
what pretraining supplies (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). And the archive's RL
scarcity is confounded with its participants' cost structure --- students on a deadline
have limited compute, time, and RL expertise --- so it records what wins under contest
constraints. Every comparison here is budget-conditional.

\subsection*{Contributions}

% Method, benchmark, comparison -- in that order, so the algorithm is the paper's
% subject and the benchmark is what it is measured on. The archive audit sits
% under the benchmark: it describes what the archive contains, and is not a
% result of comparing HL against RL.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Heuristic learning}, an agent-maintained symbolic policy under
  adversarial pressure, instrumented --- one revision per iteration, every version
  and score logged --- so that a plateau can be diagnosed and not merely observed.

  \item \textbf{A benchmark drawn from a real competition rather than built for
  evaluation}: the archive's \val{nsub} submissions and \val{nmatch} matches, plus
  the apparatus one needs --- classic games as structural controls, three opponent
  pools, a frozen evaluation set, a cross-edition Elo protocol, and a per-submission
  audit of whether it contains a learned component.

  \item \textbf{A budget-resolved comparison of HL against model-based RL, policy
  gradient, and the human field}, reported in every denominator rather than the
  favourable one: HL beats the strongest recorded human on \val{nbeat} games, but
  costs more \val{loseaxis}. Improvement frequency decays as \val{decay} and
  plateaus near \val{plateau} revisions, which we trace to maintenance capacity:
  description length there converges to \val{maintcap} within \val{maintcapspread}
  while the Elo reached varies by \val{eloplatspread}.
\end{itemize}
