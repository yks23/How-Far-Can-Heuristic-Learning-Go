\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning (RL) established itself on adversarial games. Chess,
Go, shogi, StarCraft, and Dota were where learned policies first beat the
strongest humans alive, and where the field's characteristic result took shape:
a policy trained by self-play, given enough interaction, exceeds anything a
person wrote by hand \citep{alphazero2018,alphastar2019,openaifive2019}. Games
are also where the symbolic alternative --- rules, an evaluation function, a
search procedure --- was judged to have the lower ceiling. That judgment was
formed on RL's home turf, and this paper stays there rather than retreating to
a setting chosen to favour programs.

The objection to symbolic methods is that a program exhibits only the behaviour
somebody wrote down. Every new case is another case a person has to notice,
encode, and reconcile with the rules already present, and the cost of that
reconciliation grows with the program. This is correct, and we do not dispute
it. But read it closely: it is a claim about the author, not about the artifact.
Nothing in the symbolic paradigm caps what a program can express --- the cap is
on how much of it a human will maintain before the reconciliation cost exceeds
what they can pay. Asymptotically the learned policy still wins; we are not
arguing otherwise. The question is what happens under a budget, where the
authoring cost is the binding term.

\paragraph{What coding agents changed.} They change who the author is, and the
property that matters once the author is a machine is not the one usually
argued. A symbolic program is readable, a revision is a diff, a change can be
made locally, and each edit is attributable to the behaviour it was meant to
fix; an agent accepts exactly the heterogeneous inputs that maintenance requires
--- the rules of the game, the program's own source, replays of matches it lost.
A learned policy admits no comparable loop: its behaviour lives in weights that
cannot be read, cannot be edited locally, and offer no handle by which a
specific failure traces to a specific parameter. The relevant axis is
\emph{editability}, not interpretability. Interpretability asks whether a human
can understand the policy, and a reader may reasonably not care. Editability
asks whether the optimization loop can act on the artifact at all, which for an
agent-driven loop is a precondition rather than a virtue. Programmatic RL already
describes its policies as human-readable and editable \citep{diprl2026}; what
changes here is the role the property plays --- not a feature of the delivered artifact, but the reason
an optimizer exists for it.

So the question reopens, and this time it is measurable: \emph{how far does the
symbolic paradigm go once a machine pays the maintenance cost?} The answer is
two-sided, and both sides are needed. There is a regime where symbolic policies
win, for a reason having nothing to do with expressiveness, and a point where
they stop, for a reason having nothing to do with the paradigm.

\paragraph{Why symbolic policies win under a budget.} The binding constraint
under a budget is information per sample. A scalar reward reports that an
episode went badly. The rules of the game plus a replay of the loss report what
happened, when the position turned, and which decision was responsible --- and
that difference compounds, because it determines how much of the sample survives
to the next revision. Policy-gradient methods are on-policy: a batch is used for
one update and discarded \citep{offpolicyvaluellm2026}. Off-policy methods reuse
it \citep{samplereuse2022}. A maintained program sits at the far end of the same
spectrum: it is off-policy and \emph{cross-policy-class}, able to learn from
replays of any opponent and from human source code that no gradient method can
consume, and it reads the rules directly --- a prior obtained without spending
samples at all. The effect has been seen directly: an LLM revising a Python
policy from execution traces reaches deep-RL performance on single-agent tasks at
far fewer environment interactions \citep{kuang2025generative}. That result is
directional --- one family of tasks, no shared budget accounting, no adversary
--- which is part of what this paper is for.

That spectrum also settles how the comparison must be run. Measuring against
policy gradient alone confounds symbolic-versus-neural with
on-policy-versus-off-policy, so model-based RL is our primary baseline and policy
gradient the on-policy reference point.

\paragraph{Why they stop.} What the loop accumulates is what eventually stops it.
Revisions leave the program larger and more interconnected, and each further
revision has to place an edit without disturbing what already works. Past some
size that placement fails more often than it succeeds, and improvement halts ---
not because the paradigm ran out of expressiveness, but because the artifact
outgrew what its maintainer can hold, attribute, and change.

This is measurable outside our setting, and three recent results measure it.
Coding agents extending their own prior code under an evolving specification
degrade monotonically where maintained human repositories flatten: structural
erosion rises across the large majority of trajectories, generated code runs
roughly twice as verbose as human code, and cost per iteration climbs while
correctness does not \citep{slopcodebench2026}. Decisively, quality-aware
prompting lowers the intercept and leaves the slope statistically unchanged ---
so this is a capacity limit, not a prompting defect. Two further results isolate
mechanisms behind it. Agentic repair succeeds while the accumulated context
stays small and degrades past it, and padding the context while guaranteeing that
the right files are present makes matters worse rather than better, so the limit
is not one of retrieval \citep{longcontextbugfix2026}. And given the plan and
knowledge up front, so that reasoning is not the variable under test, per-step
accuracy still falls as steps accumulate, with models more likely to err once
their own earlier errors are in context --- an effect parameter scale does not
remove \citep{longhorizonexecution2026}. A program under revision is exactly that
situation: the artifact is the accumulated context.

The first of those results cuts both ways, and the distinction is what makes our
loop tractable rather than doomed. Degradation under a long-horizon
specification is driven by that specification growing: new requirements arrive,
the program must cover them all, and accumulation is compulsory. A game's rules do not change. The agent is improving
a policy against a fixed specification, so a better heuristic may \emph{replace}
a worse one instead of being layered on top of it, and the program is free to get
shorter. Accumulation is therefore a tendency here rather than an obligation ---
which is why the ceiling is a question worth measuring instead of a foregone
conclusion, and why we log program size at every revision rather than only Elo.

The axis on which the maintenance limit falls is a description length, and it is
not the length of the rules. Go's rules fit on a page, yet no compact symbolic policy
plays it well --- the programs that do play it well carry their strength in
weights. A contest game may run to pages of special cases and still admit a
strong policy in a few hundred lines. What governs the outcome is therefore the
description length of a \emph{symbolic} policy good enough to win, which we write
$K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$ after Kolmogorov complexity and estimate through a
compression proxy, the quantity itself being uncomputable
\citep{lividanyi2019kolmogorov,rissanen1978mdl}. The prediction is a single
inequality: HL succeeds while $K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$ stays below the agent's
maintenance capacity, and plateaus as it approaches it. The program grows from a
naive draft in every game, so what differs between games is where the target sits
relative to the ceiling: where $K(\pi_{\mathrm{good}})$ lies below the capacity,
the program reaches winning strength before it becomes unmaintainable, and where
it lies above, the program stops improving while still short of it. That is what
makes the plateau diagnostic rather than incidental, and the boundary of the
paradigm follows without a separate argument. Tasks at the scale of language
modelling lie outside it not because they are adversarial or unstructured, but
because no policy of a maintainable description length is any good at them. The
ceiling is a property of the maintainer rather than of the paradigm, which is why
it moves with model capability, and why it can be measured at all.

\paragraph{Why this ceiling has not been measured.} Agent self-improvement is
already known to decay: on real engineering tasks, improvement frequency falls
roughly as $t^{-1}$ and flattens within tens of iterations
\citep{frontiereng2026}. What that does not establish is where the symbolic
paradigm stands against a learned one, because those tasks are single-agent and
carry neither a human field nor an RL baseline --- the two reference points the
question requires. Where both reference points were present, the maintainer was
always human, so the paradigm and the cost of human iteration were measured
together with no way to separate them: a heuristic program that stopped
improving might have exhausted the paradigm or exhausted its author's weekend,
and the record cannot say which. Replacing the author with an agent holds the
paradigm fixed and changes the cost of iteration --- the term that was never
varied.

\paragraph{Where to measure it.} Answering the question requires a domain where
many independent authors pushed the symbolic paradigm as far as a real budget
allowed, with the results recorded and the budget the same for every method they
might have chosen. We use the complete archive of a collegiate game
AI contest running since \val{founded}: games invented for the contest,
\val{nsub} submissions, and \val{nmatch} adjudicated matches, written by
contestants under deadline with standings at stake. Contests of this kind carry
their own folk ordering of methods --- rules for beginners, search for the
intermediate, RL as the approach with the highest ceiling --- and it is worth
naming, because it is the ordering the field's own results are taken to endorse.
What the archive records is that the same ordering gets the asymptotic ceilings
right and still fails to predict who wins:
\val{pheur} of prize-winning programs contain no learned component, and RL,
present in \val{prl} of submissions, reached the finals in \val{nrlfinal} of
\val{editions} editions.

The archive also supplies something no constructed benchmark has. Contamination
resistance is usually bought by authoring fresh tasks or by collecting new ones
continuously \citep{livecodebench2024,workbuddybench2026}; here it comes from a
venue that predates the benchmark and was never built for evaluation --- a
different provenance, which we still verify rather than assume. More
importantly, because thousands of people attempted the same games, the archive
carries a \emph{distribution} of human skill rather than a reference solution ---
so a method can be placed at a percentile of a real field, as agents have been
placed against Kaggle competitors and informatics olympiad contestants
\citep{mlebench2024,olympiad2025percentile}, instead of scored against one
baseline someone chose.

\paragraph{Heuristic learning.} We remove the confound with heuristic learning
(HL): a coding agent maintains a heuristic program by reading the rules, its own
source, and replays of its losses, revising the policy, and rescoring it against
a frozen opponent pool. The loop is deliberately narrow --- one revision per
iteration, every version and score kept --- because attribution is the property
we are trying to preserve. Its evaluator is a game engine and its opponents are
frozen, which places it at the strong end of the verification hierarchy along
which demonstrated self-improvement tracks \citep{selfimprovement2026survey};
loops that stall generally do so with weak or self-supplied evaluators.

That HL is not merely a matter of a stronger model is worth establishing early,
because the closest existing head-to-head between LLM-written agents and human
contestants in a competitive game found the humans winning: across more than
38{,}000 matches, human-written agents took the top five places, most LLM-written
agents lost to trivial baselines, and asking a frontier model to improve the
winning student's program made it substantially worse
\citep{vibecoding2025tournament}. That work generates code once, with prompting
strategies in place of a loop. We read its result as the motivation for ours: if
single-shot generation loses to undergraduates, then whatever closes the gap is
the loop, not the model --- and the loop is what this paper measures.

Two objections deserve stating before the results rather than after. The first
is that a sample-efficiency comparison between an agent and RL from scratch is
unfair, because the agent arrives with pretraining behind it. This is true, and
no accounting makes it go away: we report environment steps because they are the
resource that is scarce in a new domain, not because pretraining was free. What
can be said is narrower and is the reason the archive was chosen. The contest's
games are its own, so what pretraining plausibly supplies is general programming
competence rather than strategy for these games --- and because that is a claim
about a training corpus rather than about the venue, we test it
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}) instead of asserting it. Under that condition the
comparison is between an agent that knows how to write code and an RL system that
knows how to optimize, each starting without experience of the game in question. Readers who prefer to price
pretraining in should read our efficiency figures as conditional on a
general-purpose model already existing, which is the condition under which the
question is being asked in practice.

The second is that the archive's RL scarcity is confounded with the cost
structure of its participants: undergraduates on a deadline have limited compute,
limited time, and limited RL expertise, so RL's absence is evidence about what
wins under contest constraints, not about what RL reaches given a research budget.
We do not claim otherwise. What the archive does hold fixed is that the same
constraint applied to whichever method a contestant chose, so it records both
paradigms priced at one budget --- which is the comparison this paper is about,
and the reason the budget is stated in every result rather than assumed away.
Every comparison here is budget-conditional, and Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
reports the full ledger --- environment steps, agent calls, tokens, wall-clock,
and dollars --- including the axes on which HL is the more expensive method.

\subsection*{Contributions}

% Method, benchmark, comparison -- in that order, so the algorithm is the paper's
% subject and the benchmark is what it is measured on. The archive audit sits
% under the benchmark: it describes what the archive contains, and is not a
% result of comparing HL against RL.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Heuristic learning (HL): agent-maintained symbolic policies under
  adversarial pressure, instrumented so the ceiling can be located.} That an LLM
  can revise a program from execution feedback is established
  \citep{kuang2025generative}; what HL adds is the setting and the discipline. The
  agent reads the rules, its own source, and replays of the matches it lost,
  hypothesizes a failure mode, edits the program, and rescores against a frozen
  opponent pool --- so what comes back is not one scalar from a fixed evaluator
  but an outcome against each of many real adversaries, any of which can be
  exploited or exploit back. One revision per iteration, with every version, edit, and
  score in an append-only log, which is what lets a change in behaviour be
  attributed to the edit that caused it. That attribution is not a convenience:
  it is the only reason a plateau can be diagnosed rather than merely observed.

  \item \textbf{A benchmark drawn from a real competition rather than
  constructed for evaluation.} Its games were designed to be played and its
  submissions written to win, under deadline with standings at stake --- not
  authored as tasks, and not solved by anyone who knew a benchmark would be built
  from them. To the archive's \val{nsub} submissions and \val{nmatch} adjudicated
  matches we add the apparatus a benchmark needs: classic games as structural
  controls, three opponent pools (training, frozen test, Elo anchor), a frozen
  evaluation set, and an Elo protocol comparable across games and editions. We
  also classify every submission by whether it contains a learned component,
  which is what makes the audit above a measurement rather than an impression.

  \item \textbf{A broad comparison of HL and RL, and a mechanism for where HL
  stops.} We compare the paradigms across the benchmark's games, against
  model-based RL and policy gradient, over a range of budgets, and against the
  human field --- reporting a shared ledger in every denominator (environment
  steps, agent calls, tokens, wall-clock, dollars) rather than the one that
  favours either method. HL reaches the \val{pct}th percentile of the human field
  and surpasses the strongest recorded human on \val{nbeat} games. It is
  \val{effratio} more sample-efficient than model-based RL at matched Elo, but
  that baseline overtakes beyond \val{crossover} environment steps and HL is the
  costlier method
  \val{loseaxis}, so neither paradigm dominates: which one wins is set by the
  budget and by the game's structure. Improvement frequency decays as
  \val{decay} and plateaus near \val{plateau} revisions, and we trace that
  ceiling to maintenance capacity --- program description length at the plateau
  converges to \val{maintcap} within \val{maintcapspread} across games of
  differing complexity, which separates a limit of the agent from a limit of the
  game. The loop transfers to \val{ntransfer} further benchmarks without
  retuning.
\end{itemize}
