\section{Introduction}

Symbolic systems are widely held to lack the extensibility of connectionist ones.
A heuristic program --- rules, an evaluation function, a search procedure ---
exhibits only the behaviour somebody wrote down, whereas a policy trained by
reinforcement learning (RL) acquires behaviour its designer never specified by
interacting with the environment. The implied ordering places RL above symbolic
methods, its ceiling set by compute rather than by authorship.

That judgment is correct, and we do not dispute it. A hand-written rule set has
no mechanism for extending its own behaviour repertoire; every new case is
another case somebody has to notice, encode, and reconcile with the rules
already there. The cost of that reconciliation grows with the program, which is
the standard and accurate objection to symbolic methods. Under unbounded
interaction a learned policy should win, and asymptotically it does.

\paragraph{What coding agents changed.} The extensibility gap was never really
about expressiveness --- it was about who pays the maintenance cost. A symbolic program is exactly the kind of artifact a coding agent can
maintain: rule text and source code are readable, a revision is a diff, a change
can be made locally without disturbing the rest of the program, and each edit is
attributable to a specific behaviour it was meant to fix. Agents also accept the
heterogeneous inputs that maintenance requires --- the rules of the game, the
program's own source, and replays of the matches it lost --- and so can occupy
the role that previously required a human author. A learned policy admits no
comparable loop: its behaviour lives in weights that cannot be read, cannot be
edited locally, and offer no handle by which a specific failure can be traced to
a specific parameter. The property that matters here is editability, not
interpretability. Interpretability asks whether a human can understand the
policy, and a reader may reasonably not care. Editability asks whether the
optimization loop can act on the artifact at all, and for an agent-driven loop it
is a precondition. On this axis the symbolic/connectionist ordering reverses.

So the question is open again, and for the first time answerable: \emph{how far
does the symbolic paradigm go once the human maintenance bottleneck is removed?}

\paragraph{A setting where humans already pushed the paradigm.} Answering it
requires a domain where the symbolic paradigm was driven as hard as human authors
could drive it. We use the complete archive of a collegiate game AI contest
running since \val{founded}: \val{editions} editions, \val{nsub} submissions,
\val{nmatch} evaluated matches, with original game rules that appear in no
pretraining corpus and human code spanning a natural gradient of skill rather
than a single reference solution.

Contests of this kind carry their own folk version of the ordering above, and it
is worth naming because it shaped what the archive contains. Among participants,
writing rules is the beginner's craft, search the intermediate's, and RL the
approach with the highest ceiling --- a ranking passed along in tutorials and
postmortems, and one that contestants chose their methods by. The archive is
therefore a record of many people attempting the ladder, which is what makes it
usable as evidence.

What it records is the ordering describing asymptotic ceilings correctly while
failing to predict who wins. \val{pheur} of prize-winning programs contain no
learned component; RL appears in \val{prl} of submissions and reached the finals
in \val{nrlfinal} of \val{editions} editions. The methods ranked lowest took the
prizes, and the method ranked highest largely did not arrive.

\paragraph{Why.} Contests are decided under a budget, and under a budget the
binding constraint is not the expressiveness of the policy class but the width of
the feedback channel. A scalar reward reports that an episode went badly. The
rules of the game plus a replay of the loss report what happened, when the
position turned, and which decision was responsible. The second channel carries
far more information per sample, and when samples --- not asymptotics --- are what
is scarce, it dominates. This reframes the archive: human heuristics were not
bounded by the heuristic paradigm, they were bounded by the cost of human
iteration. The two have been confounded in every result of this kind, which is
why the paradigm's actual ceiling has never been measured.

\paragraph{Heuristic learning.} We remove the confound with heuristic learning
(HL): a coding agent maintains a heuristic program by reading the rules, its own
source, and replays of its losses, revising the policy, and rescoring it against
a frozen opponent pool. Because the archive supplies a gradient of human skill,
HL can be placed within the human field rather than compared to one baseline ---
we report the percentile of the human distribution it reaches, not a percentage
improvement over a reference implementation.

One objection deserves stating before the results rather than after. The
archive's RL scarcity is confounded with the cost structure of its participants:
undergraduates competing on a deadline have limited compute, limited time, and
limited RL expertise, so RL's absence is evidence about what wins under contest
constraints, not about what RL can reach given a research budget. We do not
claim otherwise. Every comparison in this paper is budget-conditional, and
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the full ledger --- environment steps,
agent calls, tokens, wall-clock, and dollars --- including the axes on which HL
is the more expensive method.

\subsection*{Contributions}

% Method, benchmark, comparison -- in that order, so the algorithm is the paper's
% subject and the benchmark is what it is measured on. The archive audit sits
% under the benchmark: it describes what the archive contains, and is not a
% result of comparing HL against RL.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Heuristic learning (HL), an algorithm that maintains a symbolic
  policy without a human author.} One revision per iteration: the agent reads the
  rules, its own source, and replays of the matches it lost, hypothesizes a
  failure mode, edits the program, and rescores it against a frozen opponent
  pool. Every version, edit, and score is kept in an append-only log, so a change
  in behaviour can be attributed to the edit that caused it --- the property a
  learned policy does not offer.

  \item \textbf{A benchmark drawn from a real competition rather than
  constructed for evaluation.} Its games were designed to be played and its
  submissions were written to win, by contestants under deadline with standings
  at stake --- not authored as tasks, and not solved by anyone who knew a
  benchmark would be built from them. The rules are original to the contest and
  appear in no pretraining corpus, and every match outcome is one that was
  actually adjudicated. Scale follows from the contest having run continuously
  since \val{founded}: \val{nsub} submissions and \val{nmatch} evaluated matches,
  which is what supplies a natural gradient of human skill and lets a method be
  placed at a percentile of real contestants instead of against a single
  reference. On top of the archive we add the apparatus a benchmark needs ---
  classic games as structural controls, three opponent pools (training, frozen
  test, Elo anchor), a frozen evaluation set, and an Elo protocol comparable
  across games and editions --- and we classify the submissions by whether they
  contain a learned component: \val{pheur} of prize-winning programs do not, and
  RL, present in \val{prl} of submissions, reached the finals in \val{nrlfinal}
  of \val{editions} editions.

  \item \textbf{A broad comparison of HL and RL.} We compare the two paradigms
  across the benchmark's games, against model-based RL and policy gradient, over
  a range of budgets, and against the human field --- reporting a shared ledger
  in every denominator (environment steps, agent calls, tokens, wall-clock,
  dollars) rather than the one that favours either method. Four findings. HL
  reaches the \val{pct}th percentile of the human field and surpasses the
  strongest recorded human on \val{nbeat} games. It is \val{effratio} more
  sample-efficient than RL at matched Elo, but RL overtakes beyond
  \val{crossover} environment steps and HL is the costlier method
  \val{loseaxis}, so neither paradigm dominates: which one wins is set by the
  budget and by the game's structure --- rule complexity, replay
  interpretability, state-space size, and simulation cost. HL's improvement
  frequency decays as \val{decay} and plateaus near \val{plateau} revisions, a
  ceiling we trace to \val{ceilingcause}. And the loop transfers to
  \val{ntransfer} further benchmarks without retuning.
\end{itemize}
