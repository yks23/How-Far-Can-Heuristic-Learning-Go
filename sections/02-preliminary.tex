\section{Preliminary}
\label{sec:preliminary}

This section fixes the objects and the vocabulary the rest of the paper uses: the
setting matches are played in, the two classes of policy it distinguishes, how
strength is measured against whom, and the three quantities the argument turns
on --- what a learning loop can read, what it can edit, and how long a program
has to be to win.

\paragraph{Refereed adversarial games.}
Throughout, the tasks we measure in are \emph{refereed adversarial games}, by which we
mean four conditions. The rules are fixed and fully specified in advance, so a revision
never changes what the task is. At each decision the set of legal moves is
machine-checkable, so an illegal move is detected rather than interpreted. Outcomes are
adjudicated by an external referee that the policy cannot influence, yielding a win,
loss, or draw, or a score, under a fixed round budget. And at least one participant's
behaviour lies outside the policy's control, so strength exists only relative to
opponents and never as an absolute score. A \emph{policy} is a program that reads the
state visible to its role and returns a move.

Deliberately absent from this definition are the properties usually bundled with it. It
does not require two players, a zero-sum payoff, perfect information, or symmetric
roles; the suite in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark} violates each of these somewhere, and
Section~\ref{sec:arena-provenance} says where. What the paper needs is only the four
conditions above, and it needs them because each supplies the learning loop with
something specific: fixed rules make a past revision still valid, machine-checkable
legality makes a failure attributable, an external referee makes an improvement claim
falsifiable, and an uncontrolled opponent supplies signal that does not run out. This is
also the honest reading of our scope. The four conditions are not incidental to the
result --- they are what a maintenance loop needs in order to work at all, so the class
is favourable to it, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} states what fails outside the class
rather than claiming the ceiling we measure is the paradigm's everywhere.

\paragraph{The contest archive.}
The archive we draw on is a collegiate game AI contest founded in
\val{founded} and run for \val{editions} editions, holding \val{nsub}
submissions and \val{nmatch} adjudicated matches across \val{ngames} games
authored for the contest itself. Its entrants are students, writing under
deadline with contest standings at stake; the archive records their code and
their results but not their hours, a limit that constrains what the effort
comparison in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} can claim. Alongside the venue's own
games we retain a set of classic games --- chess, Go, and others whose strategic
structure has an independent literature --- as structural controls. They control
for the risk that our complexity measurements validate only themselves: their
policy complexity can be argued about on external grounds, so they calibrate the
proxy defined below and give the cross-game ordering a reference point that our
own pipeline did not produce.

\paragraph{Symbolic and learned policies.}
A \emph{symbolic} policy carries its decision logic in source code a reader can
follow: conditions on the state, an evaluation function over positions, a search
procedure, or some composition of the three. A \emph{learned} policy carries its
behaviour in the parameters of a trained function approximator, where no
individual decision has a locatable cause in the artifact. The two are not
exclusive and most strong programs are mixtures --- a searcher with a
hand-written evaluation is symbolic in our sense, a searcher with a trained value
network is not --- so the paper treats the distinction as a property of where the
strength is stored rather than as a label on a submission. Classifying the archive
on that axis requires a decidable criterion, since the shares reported in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} are worth no more than the criterion is: a
submission counts as containing a learned component when the repository ships
parameters fitted from data, or the code that fits them, for a component that
affects move selection. Evaluation coefficients a contestant tuned by hand do not
count, however many matches informed the tuning; the contestant is the optimizer
in that loop, and treating human iteration as learning would make the category
include every serious entry and measure nothing.

\paragraph{Strength, the opponent pools, and the human state of the art.}
Because a policy's strength in a zero-sum game exists only relative to
opponents, all results are reported as Elo ratings computed from match outcomes,
never as environment returns. Two protocols appear and the paper distinguishes
them wherever a number is quoted: \emph{batch} Elo, where a set of policies is
rated jointly from a round-robin played after the fact, and \emph{online} Elo,
where a policy under revision is rated as it plays, so that its trajectory can be
read. Ratings are only comparable within a fixed set of adversaries, so three
opponent pools are kept apart. The \emph{training pool} is what a method may play
against while it improves. The \emph{frozen test pool} is fixed before any run
begins and never updated, which is what makes ratings comparable across
revisions, across methods, and across the runs of one method under different
seeds. The \emph{anchor pool}, a stratified set of archived human submissions, is
what places ratings from different games and different editions on one scale.
Against that field two distinct quantities are reported and they answer different
questions. A \emph{percentile} is the fraction of recorded human submissions to a
game that a policy outranks on the frozen pool: a statement about the
distribution, and the measurement the archive uniquely affords, since thousands
of independent attempts at the same games is what a single reference
implementation cannot supply. The \emph{human state of the art} for a game is the
single strongest submission the archive records for it, taken across all
\val{editions} editions rather than within one --- a bar that rises with every
edition, and the only reading under which the phrase is honest. Beating it is a
claim about a maximum, placing within the field is a claim about a distribution,
and Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports both because neither substitutes for
the other.

\paragraph{Editability, and how it differs from interpretability.}
Interpretability asks whether a human can understand why a policy chose what it
chose. \emph{Editability} asks something else: whether an optimizer can act on
the artifact at all --- locate the part responsible for a particular failure,
change that part, and leave the rest standing. The property is not new, and we do
not claim the word: programmatic reinforcement learning already produces policies
its authors describe as human-readable and editable~\citep{diprl2026}. What
changes here is the property's role. Where editability is ordinarily a virtue of
the deliverable, something the method provides to its eventual reader, in the
paradigm this paper studies it is a precondition for the optimizer, because the
optimizer's only move is to rewrite the artifact. The two properties come apart in
practice rather than merely in principle, which is why the distinction can be
drawn without stipulating it. A system that emits natural-language reasoning for
each decision is interpretable on any ordinary reading of the word while the
artifact its learning loop actually updates remains the
weights~\citep{tig2025think}: fully interpretable, not editable by its own loop.
The converse case is a program nobody has read.

\paragraph{The information channel.}
Editability says what a loop can change. The quantity paired with it throughout
the paper says what a loop can \emph{read} per unit of experience. A scalar return
reports that a game was lost. A loop given
the rules, its own source, and a replay of the specific loss has access to when
the position turned and which decision preceded it, which is strictly more about
the same episode. We call this the loop's \emph{information channel}, and the term
is kept separate from the representation on purpose, because the channel's width
is a property of what is available and of the agent reading it --- not of the
program being symbolic. The demonstration is a loop that holds no program at all:
rewriting failed episodes in hindsight into a memory yields the same kind of
per-sample gain~\citep{echo2025hindsight}. So the paper states two bounds over two
different objects and never merges them. The lower bound is over what the loop can
read, and the symbolic form earns no credit for it. The upper bound is over what
the loop can edit, and that is where the program is answerable.

\paragraph{Description length and the complexity axis.}
Games are ordered in this paper by the description length of a symbolic policy
good enough to win, written $K(\pi_{\text{good}})$, and the choice of that
quantity over two nearby ones is load-bearing. It is not $K(\text{rules})$, the
length of the game's specification, and it is not $K(\pi^{*})$, the length of an
optimal policy. Go separates all three: its rules fit on a page, an optimal
policy is out of reach, and --- the fact that matters here --- no compact symbolic
policy plays it well, which is why programs that play it strongly carry their
strength in weights. A game can therefore be simple to state and expensive to
play well, so rule length is the wrong axis and $K(\pi_{\text{good}})$ is the
right one. Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable and does no measuring here; it
supplies the axis and nothing else. What is measured is a compression proxy,
defined with its protocol in Section~\ref{sec:hl} and named wherever it is used,
and reported in compressed bytes rather than lines, since line counts are a
style artifact and not comparable across the languages the archive contains.

\paragraph{Budget.}
Costs are reported in three ledgers, kept separate everywhere. The
\emph{learning budget} is what a method spends improving. The \emph{evaluation
budget} is what it spends being scored, which for a method that rescores against
a frozen pool after every revision is not a rounding error. The \emph{total} is
their sum, and it is the only one of the three that may be compared across
paradigms without qualification. Each is reported in whichever units apply to the
method --- environment steps, agent invocations, tokens, wall-clock time,
currency --- because no single unit is native to both an RL learner and an agent
maintaining source code, and quoting one paradigm's favourable denominator is the
easiest way to make a budget comparison say nothing.

\paragraph{Heuristic learning and the folk ordering.}
\emph{Heuristic learning} (HL) is the paradigm in which an agent interprets
feedback and directly modifies a program, or another interpretable system, in
response; Section~\ref{sec:hl} states it precisely and separates the two halves
of the loop. Note that the definition names no game: adversarial games are the
setting in which this paper measures HL, not the scope of the paradigm. One last
term, needed because the paper tests it. Call the \emph{folk
ordering} the ranking of the three approaches by the ceiling each is expected to
reach --- rules lowest, search above them, learned policies highest. We state it
as a hypothesis rather than as a position anyone holds, and it earns its place
here by having a consequence the archive can check: if the ordering holds, the
paradigm composition of winning entries should move up it as budgets and tooling
improve across editions. Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports what
\val{editions} editions of outcomes say.
