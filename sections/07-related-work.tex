\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Adversarial games and programmatic policies.}
Reinforcement learning has become the dominant approach to adversarial games, from
value learning and self-play to imperfect-information, multiplayer, and real-time
settings \citep{dqn2015,alphago2016,alphagozero2017,alphazero2018,muzero2020,
cfr2007,deepstack2017,libratus2018,pluribus2019,alphastar2019,openaifive2019}.
Yet opponent pools remain a design choice: high average strength can hide exploitable
holes, motivating our frozen pool sampled from a human field rather than a single
reference \citep{psro2017,adversarialgo2023,asro2026coevolution,adversarialheuristics2026}.
Our comparison focuses on the small-interaction regime and uses a model-based,
off-policy baseline. A complementary line represents policies as executable programs,
using sketches, distilled trees, program search, or symbolic controllers
\citep{pirl2018,viper2018,inductivegen2020,leaps2021,symbolicpolicies2021}; recent work
formalizes their size--expressiveness trade-off \citep{programmaticrl2024foundations}.

\paragraph{Coding agents and heuristic learning.}
Language models have changed who writes such programs: they propose policies, search
structured spaces, and generate code that evolutionary or learned selectors filter
\citep{llmguidedsearch2024,qube2024,hsevo2024,diprl2026,prorl2026,teacherawareheuristic2026}.
Other agents write code around a learner, such as reward functions or training
procedures \citep{eureka2023,rewarddesignagent2026,agent2rlbench2026,ai4aibench2026};
AI4AI-Bench is complementary because it rewrites the training algorithm, whereas we
maintain the executable policy. Model-controlled program search is the closest
representation-level relative \citep{codetoplay2024,kuang2025generative}. The common
pattern treats the program as a search output; ours treats it as a persistent artifact
revised in place. Garnier et al.~call coding-agent revision of executable controllers
``heuristic learning'' \citep{garnier2026heuristic}, but study continuous control rather
than opponent-relative games, which motivates our narrower AHL scope.

\paragraph{Self-evolving agents and evaluation.}
Self-evolution can be written into language, prompts, scaffolding, or code
\citep{selfrefine2023,reflexion2023,expel2024,promptbreeder2023,textgrad2024,adas2024,
funsearch2024,stop2023,voyager2023,alphaevolve2025}. Benchmarks increasingly separate
transfer from retrieval and the agent from its harness \citep{agentcl2026,clbench2026,
harnessengineering2026}. Trajectory distillation instead compresses interaction traces
into reusable skills for retrieval and composition \citep{browserbc2026}; AHL writes
feedback directly into an executable game policy. EvoPolicyGym is the closest protocol
precedent: it evaluates repeated policy edits under a fixed episode budget with held-out
validation and trajectory diagnostics \citep{evopolicygym2026}. Its single-agent Gym
suite and checkpoint-selection objective differ from our adversarial games, frozen
opponent field, human baseline, and persistent one-artifact maintenance question. We
therefore carry the self-evolution problem into adversarial games and measure both the
performance ceiling and the maintenance capacity of an editable policy.
