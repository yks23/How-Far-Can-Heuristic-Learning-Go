\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Programmatic reinforcement learning and heuristic discovery.}
Programmatic reinforcement learning represents a policy as an executable
program rather than an opaque neural network, exposing a size--expressiveness
trade-off that is directly relevant to our description-length proxy
\citep{programmaticrl2024foundations}. Subsequent work has used language models
to synthesize such policies \citep{llmguidedsearch2024}, or searched over
structured domains such as scheduling \citep{diprl2026,prorl2026}. In parallel,
automatic heuristic-design systems have combined LLM proposals with
evolutionary selection, uncertainty balancing, diversity preservation, or
learned teachers \citep{qube2024,hsevo2024,teacherawareheuristic2026,
heuristicsearch2026,latentheuristicsearch2026,automodsat2025}. These systems
mostly optimize a fixed combinatorial objective. Our setting instead evaluates
an editable policy under adversarial play and asks how far repeated maintenance
can proceed before the artifact becomes difficult to revise.

\paragraph{LLM-guided program search and self-improvement.}
FunSearch established the now-common pattern of LLM-generated program variants
combined with an external evaluator \citep{funsearch2024}, followed by systems
such as AlphaEvolve and agentic variation operators that make proposal,
mutation, repair, and verification increasingly autonomous
\citep{alphaevolve2025,avo2026}. Other work studies recursive self-improvement
of coding agents or comparative evolution across tasks and lineages
\citep{selfimprovingcodingagent2025,darwingodelmachine2025,mendelgoedel2026}.
The closest game-specific systems use LLM-controlled program search
\citep{codetoplay2024,kuang2025generative}. We differ from these lines in
measuring a single maintained policy across many revisions, recording both its
performance and its description length, and comparing it against human and RL
reference points under a shared interaction budget.

\paragraph{Games, tournaments, and adversarial evaluation.}
Large-scale self-play and model-based planning established games as a demanding
testbed for learned agents \citep{alphazero2018,muzero2020,alphastar2019,
openaifive2019}. Recent code-agent evaluations have moved toward tournaments and
iterative competition \citep{vibecoding2025tournament,catarena2025}, while
game-theoretic co-evolution has studied LLM-based heuristic discovery against
growing strategy pools \citep{asro2026coevolution}. Adversarial-instance
generation provides a complementary direction in which the evaluator is
improved rather than the policy \citep{adversarialheuristics2026}. Our protocol
keeps the test opponent pool frozen so that changes in Elo can be attributed to
policy revisions, while the contest archive supplies a distribution of human
skill rather than a single reference opponent.

\paragraph{Experience reuse and continual learning in agents.}
Memory-based agents improve without changing the underlying program or model by
rewriting failed trajectories, retaining verbal lessons, or distilling
deployment feedback into reusable rules \citep{echo2025hindsight,reflexion2023,
learningonthejob2026}. Recent benchmarks emphasize that continual learning must
measure transfer and interference rather than only long-context retrieval
\citep{agentcl2026,clbench2026}. These approaches motivate our append-only
revision log and our separation of learning, evaluation, and total budgets, but
they do not study the maintenance of an executable heuristic under adversarial
feedback. In HL, experience is not merely retrieved: it changes the policy
artifact, which creates both the opportunity for cross-policy reuse and the
failure mode of regression through misplaced edits.

\paragraph{Long-horizon degradation and maintenance capacity.}
Several recent studies document degradation in iterative coding and agentic
execution: code erosion and verbosity increase over long coding trajectories
\citep{slopcodebench2026}, successful repair relies on decomposed short contexts
rather than simply retrieving more context \citep{longcontextbugfix2026}, and
per-step reliability remains finite even for reasoning models
\citep{longhorizonexecution2026}. Most directly, Frontier-Eng measures
self-improvement on real engineering tasks and finds that both the frequency and
the magnitude of improvements decay with continued iterations
\citep{frontiereng2026}. Frontier-Eng has no human field, RL baseline, or
relative Elo, and its evaluator is a fixed verifier; our contribution is to
carry the same longitudinal question into adversarial games and test whether a
plateau is explained by the maintainable description length of the policy rather
than by the complexity of the game rules.
