\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Programmatic reinforcement learning and heuristic discovery.}
Programmatic reinforcement learning represents a policy as an executable
program rather than an opaque neural network, exposing a size--expressiveness
trade-off that is directly relevant to our description-length proxy
\citep{programmaticrl2024foundations}. Subsequent work has used language models
to synthesize such policies \citep{llmguidedsearch2024}, or searched over
structured domains such as scheduling \citep{diprl2026,prorl2026}. In parallel,
automatic heuristic-design systems have combined LLM proposals with
evolutionary selection, uncertainty balancing, diversity preservation, or
learned teachers \citep{qube2024,hsevo2024,teacherawareheuristic2026,
heuristicsearch2026,latentheuristicsearch2026,automodsat2025}. These systems
mostly optimize a fixed combinatorial objective. Our setting instead evaluates
an editable policy under adversarial play and asks how far repeated maintenance
can proceed before the artifact becomes difficult to revise.

\paragraph{LLM-guided program search and self-improvement.}
FunSearch established the now-common pattern of LLM-generated program variants
combined with an external evaluator \citep{funsearch2024}, followed by systems
such as AlphaEvolve and agentic variation operators that make proposal,
mutation, repair, and verification increasingly autonomous
\citep{alphaevolve2025,avo2026}. Other work studies recursive self-improvement
of coding agents or comparative evolution across tasks and lineages
\citep{selfimprovingcodingagent2025,darwingodelmachine2025,mendelgoedel2026}.
The closest game-specific systems use LLM-controlled program search
\citep{codetoplay2024,kuang2025generative}. We differ from these lines in
measuring a single maintained policy across many revisions, recording both its
performance and its description length, and comparing it against human and RL
reference points under a shared interaction budget.

\paragraph{Games, tournaments, and adversarial evaluation.}
Large-scale self-play and model-based planning established games as a demanding
testbed for learned agents \citep{alphazero2018,muzero2020,alphastar2019,
openaifive2019}. Recent code-agent evaluations have moved toward tournaments and
iterative competition \citep{vibecoding2025tournament,catarena2025}, while
game-theoretic co-evolution has studied LLM-based heuristic discovery against
growing strategy pools \citep{asro2026coevolution}. Adversarial-instance
generation provides a complementary direction in which the evaluator is
improved rather than the policy \citep{adversarialheuristics2026}. Our protocol
keeps the test opponent pool frozen so that changes in Elo can be attributed to
policy revisions, while the contest archive supplies a distribution of human
skill rather than a single reference opponent.

\paragraph{Experience reuse and continual learning in agents.}
Memory-based agents improve without changing the underlying program or model by
rewriting failed trajectories, retaining verbal lessons, or distilling
deployment feedback into reusable rules \citep{echo2025hindsight,reflexion2023,
learningonthejob2026}. Recent benchmarks emphasize that continual learning must
measure transfer and interference rather than only long-context retrieval
\citep{agentcl2026,clbench2026}. These approaches motivate our append-only
revision log and our separation of learning, evaluation, and total budgets, but
they do not study the maintenance of an executable heuristic under adversarial
feedback. In HL, experience is not merely retrieved: it changes the policy
artifact, which creates both the opportunity for cross-policy reuse and the
failure mode of regression through misplaced edits.

\paragraph{Long-horizon degradation and maintenance capacity.}
Section~\ref{sec:introduction} asserts that a maintenance capacity, rather than
the expressiveness of the symbolic paradigm, is what ends the revision loop. The
mechanism is accumulation: revisions leave the program larger and more
interconnected, and each further revision has to place an edit without disturbing
what already works. Past some size that placement fails more often than it
succeeds --- not because the paradigm ran out of things to express, but because the
artifact has outgrown what its maintainer can hold, attribute, and change at once.
Recent measurements outside our setting make each term of that account concrete.
SlopCodeBench tracks 15 agents across 196 checkpoints of 36 iterative tasks and
finds erosion rising in 77\% of trajectories and verbosity in 75.5\%, with cost per
checkpoint 2.2$\times$ higher at the last checkpoint than the first ``without
corresponding improvements to correctness'' \citep{slopcodebench2026}. Against 473
open-source Python repositories the agents' code is 2.3$\times$ more verbose and
2.0$\times$ more eroded, and it accumulates both 6.6$\times$ and 5.0$\times$ faster
per revision. Human maintainers are not exempt --- 68\% of the human repositories
end more verbose than they began, median $+36\%$ --- so the distinction is a rate
(per-checkpoint slopes 0.0022 and 0.0053, against the agents' 0.0144 and 0.0264)
rather than an exemption, which is the form the claim has to take if maintenance
capacity is a general limit that agents reach sooner rather than an agent-specific
defect. That degradation also survives the obvious intervention: quality-aware
prompting lowers verbosity by 27.5--35.6\% and erosion by 34.3--57.6\% at the
intercept while leaving the growth rate intact, and pays 2.3\,pp of strict
correctness and 12.1\% additional cost for it. An intervention that buys a better
starting point, charges capability for it, and still does not bend the curve is
evidence for a capacity rather than a prompting artifact.

Two mechanisms underneath are not about code quality at all. Agentic repair
succeeds while the context each step accumulates stays small --- successful
trajectories stay under 20--30k tokens --- which locates the advantage in
decomposition rather than retrieval \citep{longcontextbugfix2026}. And given the
plan up front, per-step accuracy still falls as steps accumulate, leaving
single-turn execution horizons that differ by an order of magnitude across frontier
models \citep{longhorizonexecution2026}; scaling model size increases the
self-conditioning behind this, while reasoning removes it entirely. We cite that
mechanism and not its authors' thesis, and the direction of the reasoning result
helps us: a plateau reached by a reasoning agent cannot be attributed to
self-conditioning. A program under revision is then the case that cannot be
decomposed away, because the artifact \emph{is} the accumulated context and every
revision must hold it whole. One boundary has to be drawn here, or these results
predict that our loop cannot work at all: the degradation they measure is driven by
a specification that keeps growing, so nothing may be discarded. A game's rules do
not change, so a better heuristic may \emph{replace} a worse one and the program is
free to get shorter. Accumulation is a tendency in our setting rather than an
obligation, which is why the ceiling is worth measuring instead of assuming.

Most directly, Frontier-Eng measures self-improvement on 47 real engineering tasks
and finds decay from both directions at once: improvement frequency falls as
$t^{-1}$ and the magnitude of the $k$-th improvement as $k^{-1}$, both with
$R^2 > 0.83$, driving marginal returns near zero after 50 to 100 iterations with a
tail out to 500 \citep{frontiereng2026}. Those tasks are single-agent and carry
neither a human field nor an RL baseline, and the evaluator is a fixed verifier, so
the position of the symbolic paradigm against a learned one is left open. Where both
reference points have been present, the maintainer was always human, which measures
the paradigm and the cost of human iteration together: a heuristic program that
stopped improving might have exhausted the paradigm or its author's weekend, and the
record cannot say which. Replacing the author with an agent holds the paradigm fixed
while varying the term that never varied. A survey of this literature organizes it
by how strong a verification signal the loop closes on
\citep{selfimprovement2026survey}; a game engine with a frozen opponent pool sits at
the strong end, which is what makes the plateau attributable to the artifact rather
than to a noisy evaluator. Our contribution is to carry the same longitudinal
question into adversarial games and test whether the plateau is explained by the
maintainable description length of the policy rather than by the complexity of the
game's rules.
