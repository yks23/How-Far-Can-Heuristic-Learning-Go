\section{Adversarial Heuristic Learning (AHL)}
\label{sec:hl}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:hl-motivation}

The appeal of a symbolic paradigm begins with readability and interpretability.
A programmatic policy is intelligible to people rather than a black box: every
rule and every search decision has an explicit meaning and can be inspected or
edited locally.

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{brandaccent}{white}{\parbox{0.93\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{Observation 1: readable and intelligible.} A symbolic program policy is a
compact and highly interpretable strategy.}}
\end{center}

This advantage, however, depends on sustained heuristic maintenance by people. When new
experience reveals a failure, an expert must intervene to revise the program and
check that the change does not break behaviours that already work. As the program
grows, this maintenance requires more human labour and becomes difficult to sustain;
the bottleneck is not expressiveness but the need for people to keep maintaining it.
This limitation made reinforcement learning, which learns from feedback without
manually rewriting rules, appear to be a more attractive alternative.

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{brandaccent}{white}{\parbox{0.93\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{Observation 2: heuristic maintenance requires human labour.} A symbolic
program policy must be heuristically maintained as new experience arrives.}}
\end{center}

Coding agents built on LLMs change this trade-off. First, an LLM
operates in a language space and can directly use an interpretable symbolic policy,
attributing feedback to concrete code and mapping an observed failure to a specific
edit. Unlike reinforcement learning, which commonly receives only a scalar reward,
the agent can preserve a much wider feedback channel from trajectory to program to
policy revision. Second, the agent can repeatedly observe and revise in natural
language, supplying the sustained maintenance that the original heuristic paradigm
required from humans.

The benefit is especially consequential in adversarial environments. Different
opponents induce substantially different behaviours and effective environments,
creating a highly dynamic, large search space in which each pattern is observed only
through sparse samples. This diversity places unusually strong demands on robustness:
the learner must use each informative interaction and transfer what it learns across
opponents. Maintaining an executable program through an agent is therefore a natural
fit for this setting.

\begin{center}
\fcolorbox{brandaccent}{white}{\parbox{0.93\linewidth}{\small
\textbf{Observation 3: coding agents exploit and compensate.} Coding agents can
understand and maintain symbolic program policies, turning feedback into concrete
revisions while reducing the human labour required for heuristic maintenance.}}
\end{center}

under the sparse and shifting feedback of adversarial games, this agent--program
loop converts that information into measurable policy improvement.

\subsection{Introducing Adversarial Heuristic Learning}
\label{sec:hl-method}

\begin{definition}[Adversarial game]
An adversarial game is an interactive environment
\[
\mathcal{G}=\bigl(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B},\mathcal{O},P,R\bigr),
\]
where the policy acts with $a_t\in\mathcal{A}$, an external opponent acts with
$b_t\in\mathcal{B}$, and the transition or outcome distribution
$\bigl(P,R\bigr)(\cdot\mid s_t,a_t,b_t,o)$ depends on the opponent
$o\in\mathcal{O}$. Each round selects an opponent subset
$\mathcal{O}_t\subseteq\mathcal{O}$, which determines the behaviours and feedback
observed in that round.
\end{definition}

We use \emph{Adversarial Heuristic Learning} (\ahl) for the following setting:
an agent iteratively edits one executable policy, receives decision-level traces
from matches against changing external opponents, and is evaluated after training
against a referee-side evaluation pool it cannot rewrite. AHL is therefore the adversarial-game
instantiation studied here, rather than a definition of heuristic learning in
general. The policy artifact persists across rounds; each revision is made from
the current program, the game rules, and the feedback collected so far.

The instantiation is a loop between two agents, and it is deliberately simple. A
\emph{policy agent} is the program under revision; it plays the game and produces
interaction traces. A \emph{generative agent} observes those traces, together with
the rules and the policy agent's own source, and either generates a policy or
revises the one in hand. Its output is the next version of the policy agent, and
the loop repeats.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:loop} states it in full.
The complete overview, including the contest-game suite in which the loop is measured,
is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hl-arena-overview}.

\begin{algorithm}[b]
\caption{Adversarial heuristic learning. A generative agent observes the interaction between
the policy agent and its environment, then generates or revises the policy agent.
Each round selects an opponent subset and returns a revised policy.}
\label{alg:loop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require game rules $R$, opponent pool $\mathcal{O}$, budget $B$
  \State $\pi_0 \gets \Call{Generate}{R}$
    \Comment{no policy in hand: the agent writes one}
  \For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $B$}
    \State $\mathcal{O}_t \gets \Call{SelectOpponents}{\mathcal{O}}$
      \Comment{$\mathcal{O}_t \subseteq \mathcal{O}$; size is a design choice}
    \State $\tau_t \gets \Call{Play}{\pi_{t-1}, \mathcal{O}_t}$
      \Comment{states, actions, and final outcomes}
    \State $\pi_t \gets \Call{Revise}{\pi_{t-1}, R, \textsc{src}(\pi_{t-1}), \tau_t}$
      \Comment{reads; then edits}
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\pi_B$
    \Comment{evaluation is performed after training}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

We use one revision per iteration; evaluation scores are measured after training,
not supplied to the agent during this loop.

\subsection{Key Elements of AHL}
\label{sec:hl-bottleneck}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:loop} has three elements that determine what AHL can learn:
the opponents, the feedback information, and the maintenance rule. They determine
which behaviours are exposed, how much of each interaction reaches the agent, and
whether that information can be written into the persistent program.

\paragraph{Opponents.}
Each round selects an opponent subset from the available pool. The subset may be
the full pool or a smaller set, and its composition determines which behaviours and
failure modes the policy encounters. A narrow or repetitive subset can leave parts
of the policy untested; a broader or changing subset supplies more diverse evidence
but divides the available samples across more strategies.

\paragraph{Feedback information.}
The feedback contract controls the input channel. As illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:feedback-width}, outcome-only feedback forces the
agent to guess which rule caused a loss, whereas decision-level traces expose the
sequence of actions, opponent replies, and the turning point needed to edit a
specific rule. In adversarial games, the opponent subset is part of this contract:
it may be the full pool or a smaller selected set, and the agent can only learn from
failures that those opponents elicit. Thus both who the policy plays and what each replay contains determine the information
available for the next revision; our concrete trace is specified in
Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}.

\paragraph{Maintenance rule.}
The rule controls how revisions are proposed, checked, accepted, and remembered.
It must prevent a fix for one opponent from silently breaking behaviour that already
works against others, while retaining rejected changes so that the loop does not
rediscover them. Recording each revision and its feedback makes the maintenance
process attributable; its stability is an empirical question evaluated in
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.
