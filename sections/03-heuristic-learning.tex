\section{Heuristic Learning}
\label{sec:hl}

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:hl-motivation}

Two properties made the symbolic paradigm attractive and one retired it. A program
states a decision boundary directly: a conditional test or a search cutoff
expresses in one line what a network approximates with a great many parameters,
and the line can be read, argued with, and changed. Figure~\ref{fig:size} places
the two paradigms on the axis this paper cares about. A symbolic policy competent
at one of these games occupies \val{progsize}; a learned policy of comparable
strength occupies \val{netsize}. The figure is not an argument that smaller is
better --- given enough interaction the learned policy still wins, and this paper
does not dispute that. It is an argument that the two paradigms sit at points on
the description-length axis separated by orders of magnitude, which is what makes
that axis worth measuring rather than assuming.

That compactness is what makes the program space a convenient destination for
human experience. A person who has understood something about a game --- that a
particular structure is worth material, that an opening line loses --- can write
it down as a rule and have it take effect immediately, with no training run in
between and no question of whether the lesson survived the optimization. The
archive this paper draws on is thousands of such acts of writing down, which is
why it can be read as a record of what the paradigm reached rather than only as a
set of programs.

What the paradigm did not have is a maintainer who could keep paying, and it is
worth being precise about where the payment goes. The expensive part of adding a
rule is not writing it; it is establishing that it does not break the rules already
there. That is why the cost grows with the program rather than staying flat: each
addition has to be checked against a larger body of existing behaviour, so the same
edit costs more late than early. Where revision stops has therefore always been a
fact about the person doing it rather than about what a program can express.

In the agent era the constraint changes character, because a coding agent's input
and output space is program text. It does not need the program translated into
some other representation to act on it: the artifact it reads is the artifact it
writes. The same holds on the feedback side --- an interaction trace, a replay of
a lost game, is also text an agent can read directly, against the program that
produced it. So both halves of the maintenance loop run in a space the agent
operates in natively, and the loop can carry far more per unit of experience than
a scalar reward: not that the game was lost, but which decision preceded the
loss and which rule was responsible. What this buys is a question for
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} rather than a claim to make here; the architectural
point is only that the channel is wide and that nothing in the loop has to
translate between representations for it to close.

\subsection{Introducing heuristic learning}
\label{sec:hl-method}

\emph{Heuristic learning} is the paradigm in which an agent interprets feedback
and directly modifies a program, or another interpretable system, in response. The
definition names no game and no game-specific machinery: adversarial games are
where this paper measures HL, not the boundary of the paradigm. Its two halves are
the two properties Section~\ref{sec:preliminary} defined --- what the loop can
read, and what it can edit --- and they are one object here, because a loop that
reads without editing is analysis and a loop that edits without reading is search.

The instantiation is a loop between two agents, and it is deliberately simple. A
\emph{policy agent} is the program under revision; it plays the game and produces
interaction traces. A \emph{generative agent} observes those traces, together with
the rules and the policy agent's own source, and either generates a policy or
revises the one in hand. Its output is the next version of the policy agent, which
is then rescored against the frozen test pool, and the loop repeats.
Figure~\ref{fig:loop} shows the arrangement.

Two constraints on the loop are worth stating because they cost something. One
revision per iteration, and an append-only log of every version, score, and trace:
a faster loop would batch several edits per rescoring, and we give that up to keep
attribution, because a plateau that cannot be traced to particular revisions can
be observed but not diagnosed, and diagnosing it is what this paper is for. The
generative agent's two modes --- generating a policy where there is none, revising
one that exists --- are also kept distinct in the log, since proficiency at the
first is not evidence of skill at the second and the two are not strongly
correlated in existing measurements of code agents on iterated
tournaments~\citep{catarena2025}.
