\subsection{From heuristic learning to adversarial heuristic learning}
\label{sec:hl-method}

\emph{Heuristic learning} is an umbrella term rather than a settled single task
definition. Recent uses include hierarchical norm updates and coding agents that revise
executable feedback laws under simulator budgets~\citep{yang2018hierarchical,garnier2026heuristic}.
We use the narrower name \emph{Adversarial Heuristic Learning} (\ahl): an agent
iteratively edits one executable policy, receives decision-level traces from matches
against external opponents, and is scored by a referee-side evaluation pool it cannot
rewrite. Thus AHL is not a claim about HL in general; it is the adversarial-game
instantiation studied here. Its two halves are

% two properties are now defined in 3.1 above -- editability in the paragraph on the agent
% era, the channel at its end -- so the reference is backwards within this same section.
the two properties Section~\ref{sec:hl-motivation} set out --- what the loop can
read, and what it can edit --- and they are one object here, because a loop that
reads without editing is analysis and a loop that edits without reading is search.

The instantiation is a loop between two agents, and it is deliberately simple. A
\emph{policy agent} is the program under revision; it plays the game and produces
interaction traces. A \emph{generative agent} observes those traces, together with
the rules and the policy agent's own source, and either generates a policy or
revises the one in hand. Its output is the next version of the policy agent, which
is then rescored against the frozen test pool, and the loop repeats.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:loop} states it in full.
The complete overview, including the contest-game suite in which the loop is measured,
is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hl-arena-overview}.

\begin{algorithm}[b]
\caption{Heuristic learning. A generative agent observes the interaction between
the policy agent and its environment, then generates or revises the policy agent.
One revision per iteration; every version, score, and trace is appended to the log.}
\label{alg:loop}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
  \Require game rules $R$, training pool $\mathcal{O}_{\text{train}}$, frozen test
    pool $\mathcal{O}_{\text{test}}$, budget $B$
  \State $\pi_0 \gets \Call{Generate}{R}$
    \Comment{no policy in hand: the agent writes one}
  \State $\mathcal{L} \gets \langle\,\rangle$
    \Comment{append-only log}
  \For{$t = 1$ \textbf{to} $B$}
    \State $\tau_t \gets \Call{Play}{\pi_{t-1}, \mathcal{O}_{\text{train}}}$
      \Comment{interaction traces, including the losses}
    \State $\pi_t \gets \Call{Revise}{\pi_{t-1}, R, \textsc{src}(\pi_{t-1}), \tau_t}$
      \Comment{reads; then edits}
    \State $e_t \gets \Call{Score}{\pi_t, \mathcal{O}_{\text{test}}}$
      \Comment{Elo on the frozen pool}
    \State append $(\pi_t, e_t, \tau_t)$ to $\mathcal{L}$
  \EndFor
  \State \Return $\mathcal{L}$
    \Comment{the trajectory is the result, not $\pi_B$ alone}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Two constraints on the loop are worth stating because they cost something. One
revision per iteration, and an append-only log of every version, score, and trace:
a faster loop would batch several edits per rescoring, and we give that up to keep
attribution, because a plateau that cannot be traced to particular revisions can
be observed but not diagnosed, and diagnosing it is what this paper is for. The
generative agent's two modes --- generating a policy where there is none, revising
one that exists --- are also kept distinct in the log, since proficiency at the
first is not evidence of skill at the second and the two are not strongly
correlated in existing measurements of code agents on iterated
tournaments~\citep{catarena2025}.

\subsection{Where the loop binds}
\label{sec:hl-bottleneck}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:loop} has two lines that decide what the paradigm can do, and
the rest is bookkeeping. One is the trace the loop observes; the other is the rule
under which it revises. They correspond to the paradigm's two halves --- reading
and editing --- so a limit in either is a limit on the whole loop, and neither is
fixed by the definition. Both are choices, and both cost something.

\paragraph{The observation trace.}
What $\tau_t$ contains sets an upper limit on what any revision can be responsible
for. A trace that reports only outcomes leaves the generative agent to guess which
part of the program was at fault, and a loop that guesses is doing search with
extra steps; a trace that carries the sequence of decisions, the opponent's
replies, and the point at which the position turned lets it attribute a loss to a
rule. This is the same quantity Section~\ref{sec:hl-motivation} argued the
representation makes available, seen from the other side: the channel being wide
in principle does not mean the loop is using it, because the width is a property of
what the harness records. Two things bound it in practice. The trace competes with
the program for the agent's attention, since both must be held at once to connect
a failure to the line that produced it, and traces of full games are long ---
against which the relevant external evidence is that successful agentic repair
trajectories stay well under the context limits available to
them~\citep{longcontextbugfix2026}. And in an adversarial setting the trace can
only exhibit failures that some opponent elicits, so the pool the policy agent
plays against determines which parts of the program ever come under scrutiny. What
our traces contain is stated with the apparatus in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark};
the point here is that it is a design variable rather than a given.

\paragraph{The revision rule.}
Reading a trace well does not make a trajectory improve. The difficulty is not
expressing a fix --- a program can express it, which is
Section~\ref{sec:hl-motivation}'s first claim --- but that an edit which corrects
an observed loss may disturb behaviour that was already working, and nothing in
the loop as stated prevents this. Three properties of the revision rule decide how
often that happens: how much of the program a single revision may touch, whether a
revision is accepted on evidence or simply appended, and what the loop remembers
about revisions it has already tried and abandoned. The third is the one most
easily left out, and its cost is visible in existing systems: in evolutionary code
search \val{evotracecycle} of added lines are byte-identical
re-introductions of previously deleted ones, in nearly every
run~\citep{evotrace2026}. That setting maintains a population rather than one
artifact, so the rate is not a prediction for our loop; what it establishes is
that a loop keeping no record of what it has rejected will spend budget rediscovering
it. These are the properties on which the stability of the trajectory depends, and
whether ours is stable is a measurement rather than an argument ---
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} reports the spread.

The two bottlenecks pull against each other, which is why they are stated
together. The discipline that keeps revision stable is to rescore each version
against a fixed reference and keep or discard it on the result, and that reduces a
